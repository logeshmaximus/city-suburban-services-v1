What is a ductless mini-split, and how does it heat and cool without ducts?

SHORT ANSWER

A ductless mini-split is two pieces: an outdoor compressor unit and one or more indoor heads, joined by a copper refrigerant line set instead of ductwork. The system moves heat in either direction, so the same equipment cools in summer and heats in the colder months. City & Suburban installs and repairs ductless systems in Chicago homes where no ducts exist, or where adding them would mean losing ceiling height.

Picture a rehabbed attic in a Logan Square two-flat. The space gets steam heat from the building below and a window unit wedged into a dormer. In July the room runs eight degrees warmer than the floor beneath it. In March the heat arrives whether the room needs it or not. No duct reaches the third floor, and adding one would cut into the only closet. This is the room a mini-split was built for, and the one case where cooling services and heating arrive in the same cabinet.

The usual fix is a stack of appliances. A window unit for summer, a space heater for winter, a box fan to push air down the stairs. The cost lands in three places. The window is blocked from May to October, and the noise sits in the room. The electric bill climbs too, because resistance heat uses more electricity than a heat pump for the same warmth and a single-speed window unit cycles at full output. Some of these appliances have thermostats, but each serves only the spot where it sits.

What changed is the compressor. An inverter-driven compressor varies its speed instead of cycling on and off, so a mini-split can hold a room within a degree at part load. The line set, usually two insulated copper tubes and a control cable, replaces the duct entirely. A reversing valve sends heat the other way in winter, pulling warmth out of cold outdoor air. One cabinet outside, both seasons, controlled room by room.

A ductless quote starts as a room question before an equipment question. Which rooms are uncomfortable, what the walls are made of, where an outdoor unit can sit, how far the line set has to run. That count covers houses and small multi-family buildings, not large apartment buildings. Ductless is not a niche product in this city. For much of the housing stock it is the only route to cooling that does not start with new ductwork.

Which Chicago buildings end up with no ductwork, and where does a mini-split fit?

SHORT ANSWER

Much of Chicago's older housing stock heats with steam or hot water and may have no usable air ducts. Additions, attics and coach houses were finished long after the mechanical layout was set. City & Suburban installs ductless systems in exactly those spaces: third-floor conversions, garden units, sunrooms and rooms over a garage. Condos where a shaft wall blocks every duct route are the other common case.

Four building types come up again and again. The pre-1940 greystone or two-flat with steam and hot-water heat and often no usable duct in the building. The bungalow with a finished attic above a single trunk line. The condo carved out of a courtyard building, where the only wall facing outside is the one with the windows. And the rear addition, built decades later onto a house whose mechanical room is forty feet away.

The default answer is to extend the ducts. In a masonry building that means a soffit down a hallway, a chase cut through a closet, or joists drilled where drilling becomes a structural question. The homeowner pays twice: once for carpentry and patching that adds no comfort, and again in lost space. In a condo, the board has to approve the work before any of it starts.

A single-zone mini-split often needs only a small sleeve through one wall, sized to the manufacturer's line set and drain requirements. Multiple heads, fire-rated walls and condensate routes can need more penetrations or more work. Where the line set, control cable and drain share one hole, it is pitched down to shed water. This changes which rooms are candidates. A finished attic, a garden-level office or a four-season room can be conditioned without touching the rest of the building. The existing system is not asked to carry a load it was not sized for.

The building decides the system here, because the wall, the drain path and the outdoor location set the limits of what can be installed. Single-zone or multi-zone: how many ductless heads does a home need?

SHORT ANSWER

One head conditions one open space. It does not push air around a corner or up a stairwell. A single-zone system suits one room or a small open floor. A multi-zone system runs several heads off one outdoor unit, each with its own control; how many depends on the model, its minimum modulation and its shared-mode limits. City & Suburban prices ductless work upfront with no hidden fees, so the cost of a second or third zone is clear before the job starts.

The first request is almost always for one large head to handle a whole floor. In a 1920s Chicago layout with a center hallway and doors on both sides, that does not work. Conditioned air follows the shortest return path, so the hallway reaches the setting, the head satisfies and shuts down, and the two bedrooms stay as warm as before. Open-plan rehabs are the exception, not the rule.

The common response is to oversize. A head rated well above the room's load feels safer, and one zone is a cheaper quote than three. The cost arrives later. An oversized head reaches the setting fast, drops to its minimum output, and stops pulling humidity out of the air, so the room feels clammy at 72 degrees. Short cycling also wears the parts that cycle.

The mechanism most quotes skip is minimum capacity. A multi-zone outdoor unit has a floor below which it cannot modulate, and every head draws from that same compressor. Three small heads on a large outdoor unit can leave the system running above the load of any single room. Matching each head to its room, and the sum of the heads to the outdoor unit, is the real design work. The same modulation limits show up in any heat pump service call on a ducted system.

Zone layout is the decision that comes before equipment selection on this kind of job. A two-zone system with correctly matched heads usually holds temperature better than a three-zone system built around one oversized head. The usual plan treats ductless as a summer purchase and ignores the heating half. The head gets sized for the cooling load, which in Chicago is the smaller of the two loads. What usually goes wrong with a ductless mini-split, and what fixes it?

SHORT ANSWER

Most ductless service calls come down to airflow or water rather than refrigerant. The usual causes are a fouled blower wheel, a blocked drain, a dirty outdoor coil, or a communication fault between head and outdoor unit. City & Suburban repairs all makes and models, diagnoses before anything is quoted, and offers same-day service when the schedule allows.

Two complaints dominate ductless calls. A head that runs but no longer cools, and a head that drips down the wall in July. Both tend to show up in the third or fourth summer. Both get lived with for a few weeks first, because a mini-split failing slowly is easy to ignore.

The common assumption is low refrigerant. A sealed system does not consume refrigerant, so a system that is low has a leak, and adding more without finding the leak buys one season. The other default move is a filter rinse. Rinsing the two mesh filters takes five minutes and helps, but it does not reach the part that actually fouls.

That part is the blower wheel. A ductless head pulls room air across a small barrel fan and a cold coil, and over years a film builds on the blades and the coil fins. Airflow falls, the coil runs colder, condensate misses the pan, and water finds the wall. The same film is what makes a head smell on startup. A ducted system fails differently, which is why an AC repair call follows another sequence.
