Why does the air conditioner turn on and off every few minutes?

SHORT ANSWER

Short cycling means the system is being stopped early, either by its controls or by a safety switch. Cycle timing is worth noting, but timing alone does not identify the fault. Pressures read through a full cycle, with control and wiring checks, show whether a pressure switch is tripping or a control is dropping out. Refrigerant added without that data can hide the fault. City & Suburban diagnoses the cause before anything is quoted.

In a West Loop condo the system starts, cools for three or four minutes, then stops. It restarts a few minutes later and does the same thing again. The thermostat sits on an interior wall near the kitchen, where cooking heat reaches it first. Rooms furthest from the air handler never catch up, so the setpoint goes lower and the cycling gets faster.

The common response is a new thermostat. Sometimes the setpoint is simply dropped two degrees. Either change can quiet the symptom for a day, and neither touches a safety switch that is doing exactly what it was built to do. Condenser cleaning and filter work belong in a scheduled AC tune-up, not in a second paid visit for the same symptom.

Short cycling comes from three different places. A control fault means the thermostat is reading the wrong air, or a wire is intermittent, so the call for cooling drops out. A protection trip means a pressure switch or an overload shuts the compressor down, usually after a dirty coil or a low charge. Oversized capacity means the system satisfies the thermostat faster than the rooms can mix the air.

City & Suburban diagnoses the cause before anything is quoted. Short cycles with cold air at the register can come from a control fault, but measured checks have to confirm it. A cycle that ends with the outdoor unit stopping, while the indoor fan runs on, can indicate a protection trip. Coil condition, the filter and the wiring at both ends all belong in the same diagnostic.

Intermittent wiring is a textbook cause, and it rarely shows up on a single meter reading.   • A cycle that ends with the outdoor unit stopping while the indoor fan runs on: a pressure switch or an overload may be tripping.
  • Cycling that began right after a thermostat swap: the new control may be wired for a different system.
  • Cycling only on hot afternoons: condenser airflow and refrigerant charge are the first suspects.
  • Cycling plus ice on the copper line: treat it as an airflow or charge fault, not a control fault.
  • Safe checks at home: change the air filter, clear leaves and debris from around the outdoor unit, and confirm nothing blocks the return grille.

What causes a frozen AC coil, and what should happen first?

SHORT ANSWER

Ice forms when the evaporator coil runs below freezing, which happens with too little airflow across it or too little refrigerant inside it. The first step is to stop cooling so the coil can thaw, because readings taken on ice mean nothing. Running the fan alone helps only when the equipment is dry and the manual allows it; with overflow or water near wiring, switch the system off and keep away. Airflow and charge then decide which side caused the ice. City & Suburban diagnoses the cause before anything is quoted.

A Logan Square basement in August, and the copper line leaving the air handler is wearing a white sleeve of frost. Upstairs the air is barely moving. Shut the system off and the ice melts, so water runs across the floor beside the drain pan. Restart it and the frost is back within the hour.

The frequent response is a pound of refrigerant, a scraped coil, or both. A low charge does cause ice, so the symptom sometimes clears for a while. A clogged filter and a blocked return cause identical ice. Refrigerant added to a system that was never low leaves it overcharged for the rest of its life.

Ice is a symptom of heat starvation at the coil. Refrigerant boils inside the coil at a temperature set by pressure, and when warm indoor air stops arriving, that temperature falls below freezing. Condensation then turns to frost, the frost blocks what airflow was left, and the problem accelerates.
