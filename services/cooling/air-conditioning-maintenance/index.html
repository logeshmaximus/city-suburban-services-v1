Equipment types

A tune-up looks beyond the outdoor cabinet.

Outdoor air conditioner

The condenser releases indoor heat to outdoor air. Coil condition and clear airflow matter when cottonwood and street dust collect around the cabinet. Buildup between the fins makes heat release harder even when the unit still runs.

Indoor evaporator coil

The evaporator coil transfers heat out of indoor air and collects moisture during cooling. Filter condition, coil cleanliness and drainage deserve attention together. Confirm what can be inspected without assuming every enclosure offers the same access.

Air handler

The air handler moves air through the indoor cooling system. Filter fit and a clean air path help the evaporator coil receive airflow. Dust on the blower wheel can restrict air movement even after a fresh filter is installed.

Furnace-connected cooling

Some central AC systems use a furnace for indoor air movement. The shared filter and blower still matter in summer. Dust left from heating season can restrict cooling airflow, so the indoor air path needs attention alongside the outdoor coil.

What does an air conditioning tune-up actually cover?

SHORT ANSWER

A tune-up is half cleaning and half measurement. City & Suburban's technicians are NATE-certified, and a thorough visit washes the outdoor coil, flushes the condensate drain, and inspects the evaporator coil and filter. Refrigerant pressures and the temperature split across the coil get measured, and the electrical side is tightened and tested. The useful output is a set of readings rather than a row of checkmarks, because readings can be compared next spring.

Most Chicago cooling systems live in two tight places at once. The condenser sits on a flat roof, in a gangway, or bolted to a back deck, while the evaporator coil sits on top of the furnace in the basement. Both halves collect a season of dust while nothing runs. Cooling and heating share that cabinet in most homes, which is why cooling services and furnace work land on the same equipment.

The usual spring visit is a filter change, a quick look at the outdoor unit, and a note that the system is cooling. It takes twenty minutes and produces no numbers. Nothing in that visit would catch a charge that has drifted low, a capacitor reading below its rating, or a drain that is half full of biofilm. The homeowner pays for an inspection and receives a reassurance.

Cooling faults build slowly and show up all at once. A coil that loses airflow raises operating pressure, the compressor runs hotter, and capacity falls off before anyone notices a comfort problem. By the time rooms stop cooling, the part that was merely stressed in May may already have failed. Measurement is what separates the two states, since a stressed system and a healthy one look identical from the thermostat.

City & Suburban's technicians are NATE-certified. A good tune-up is a data visit: the charge is checked against measured pressures and temperature split instead of by feel, and the electrical readings are taken under load. At the end of a good visit the homeowner should have the measured readings, the filter size, and a written price for anything found weak; confirm before booking which readings the visit includes. A system that has already stopped cooling is a different job and belongs with AC repair.

What do cottonwood and city grime do to an outdoor AC unit?

SHORT ANSWER

Both block the coil the system uses to dump heat. Cottonwood seed in June, dryer lint in a gangway, and brake dust along a busy street form a felt mat across the fins. A thorough tune-up should rinse that mat out from the inside of the coil, using a method the manufacturer allows; confirm coil cleaning is in the visit scope. A blocked condenser raises operating pressure and cuts cooling capacity long before the unit stops running.

June in Chicago fills the air with cottonwood seed, and the outdoor unit works as a filter for the whole block. Add the particular places these units sit. A gangway three feet wide, a flat roof near a vent fan, a back deck under a dryer exhaust, a pad in the alley. Each one feeds the coil something different. The fins hold it, and the next rain packs it tighter.

The usual answer is a pressure washer or a hose aimed hard at the fins. A heavy spray drives the mat deeper into the fin pack, where it sets, and flattens the fins. Covers make it worse by trapping moisture against the coil all winter. The homeowner sees a clean-looking cabinet, the coil is still blocked, and the first evidence is a system that cannot hold temperature on a 95-degree day.

The mechanism is simple heat transfer. A condenser rejects the heat pulled out of the house, and air has to move through the fins to carry it away. When that airflow drops, head pressure climbs, the compressor draws more current and runs hotter, and capacity falls. Sustained high pressure is what kills compressors and what pushes a marginal refrigerant leak open at a joint or a valve.

A proper condenser cleaning works from the inside out, straightens bent fins, checks the clearance, and clears the base pan on roof-mounted units. That order matters, because the mat comes out of the coil instead of setting deeper into it. Technicians are NATE-certified, and City & Suburban repairs all makes and models, not only the American Standard equipment it installs.

A blocked and dirty coil also hides leaks. Brian K. wrote in March 2024 that a City & Suburban technician "found a leak in my air conditioning condenser the other company had missed multiple times." Where a tune-up finds an active leak rather than dirt, the work moves to air conditioning repair.

Pro tip: Keep three feet of clear air around the condenser and nothing stored on top of it. After cottonwood season a homeowner can shut the unit off at the disconnect and rinse the coil gently from the outside. Use a garden hose, never a pressure washer, and leave cabinet work to the technician. A dryer vent pointed at the coil should be redirected.

What does skipping AC maintenance cost a Chicago home?

SHORT ANSWER

It costs efficiency first and the compressor last. A dirty coil, a low charge and a starved filter each make the system run longer for the same cooling, which shows up on summer bills. The failures that follow land on the hottest days. A measured spring visit can catch many of those conditions as readings, while they are still adjustments rather than replacements.

A Chicago cooling system has a short, brutal duty cycle. Cook County Assessor records put the median construction year for houses and small multi-family buildings in the city's townships at 1927. That housing stock means retrofitted ducts, tight mechanical rooms and long line sets. Such a system already works harder than the rating on the data plate suggests. Neglect takes away the margin left at installation.

Deferred maintenance is usually framed as a saving. The math looks right for a year or two, and then stops. Running undercharged wears a compressor continuously, a blocked drain finds a ceiling, and a capacitor that drifted out of range in May can fail in July at emergency-call timing. The visit that was skipped cost a fraction of the part, and none of the three faults announced itself in advance.

The reason neglect goes unnoticed is that air conditioners degrade gracefully. Capacity falls off in small steps, and a thermostat set to 72 that reaches 76 on a hot afternoon reads as weather rather than as a fault. Efficiency losses arrive the same way, spread across a utility bill with no line item attached. Only measured readings catch the drift, which is why the numbers matter more than the cleaning.

The readings that show drift are pressures, temperature split, capacitor values and amp draw, and those readings are worth recording every year. A charge that is low gets investigated as a leak rather than topped off and forgotten, since refrigerant does not get consumed. How is refrigerant charge checked during a tune-up?

SHORT ANSWER

With gauges and a thermometer, not by topping off. A sound charge check measures suction and liquid pressure, calculates superheat or subcooling against the outdoor temperature, and reads the split between supply and return air. Ask which of these readings the visit includes. Those readings say whether the charge is correct, whether airflow is the real problem, and whether refrigerant has been leaking out of a sealed system.

Refrigerant does not get used up. A sealed system holds the same charge for twenty years unless something opens a path. A line set rubbing in a joist bay, a corroded coil, a loose flare at a service valve. Chicago line sets often run a long way from a basement coil up to a roof or a rear pad. Distance and vibration give a leak plenty of places to start.

The quick version of a charge check is a can of refrigerant and a gauge on the low side. Pressure comes up, air at the register feels colder, and the invoice says the system was low. Nothing in that sequence identifies where the refrigerant went. The same amount leaves again over a season or two, and the second visit costs what the first one did.

Two things changed. Current systems run at pressures where guessing fails, so charge has to be set by weight or against a measured subcooling target with little room either side. And a low charge is now read as a symptom with a cause attached. Finding the leak is the step that a pressure reading on its own skips entirely.

The right order is to measure before touching a valve and to treat an undercharged system as a leak until the search says otherwise. Readings should be taken with the system running under load, which is the only state that tells the truth. Why do AC condensate drains clog, and what stops it?

SHORT ANSWER

Because the line carries warm, wet condensate and return-air dust in the dark. Biofilm builds inside the trap until water backs up into the pan. A cooling tune-up should flush the line, test the float switch and check the pan, and City & Suburban's technicians are NATE-certified. A blocked drain is one of the most common ways a working cooling system damages a Chicago home.

A cooling system pulls water out of the air all summer. Gallons a day in humid weather drip off the evaporator coil into a pan, then run to a floor drain, a utility sink or a condensate pump. In a Chicago basement that line is often long, nearly flat and tucked above a finished ceiling. In a condo it may cross a closet and end at a stack.

The drain gets attention when water appears. By then a stain has spread on the ceiling below, or the cabinet is standing in a puddle, and the repair bill is drywall rather than plumbing. A wet-vac clears the blockage for that season. Nothing has been done about the slope, the missing trap, or the float switch that never tripped.

The mechanism is biological rather than mechanical. Condensate is warm and carries dust from the return air, which feeds a slime layer on the inside of the pipe. That layer narrows the line steadily over a season, so the clog is predictable in a way a random failure is not. A planned flush clears it before the weather turns humid.

A cooling tune-up should flush the drain line and trap, test the float switch, and check the pan for rust. Where a condensate pump sits in the path, the pump and its check valve deserve a test as well. A float switch that works turns an overflow into a stopped system rather than a stained ceiling. Where the indoor coil sits on the furnace, checks on both halves of the air conditioning and heating system can share one visit only if that scope and its price are agreed at booking; a cooling tune-up is not automatically a heating inspection.

  • Flush the drain line and trap at the start of cooling season, not after water appears.
  • Confirm the pan drains toward its outlet and holds no water left over from last summer.
  • Test the float switch by lifting it: cooling should stop rather than keep making water.
  • Check the condensate pump, its check valve and the discharge tubing wherever one is used.
  • Look for old stains on the ceiling or floor below the coil, which mark a drain that has backed up before.

How do the filter, blower and electrical parts decide whether cooling holds?

SHORT ANSWER

Airflow and voltage do the quiet work. A starved filter or a dust-loaded blower wheel drops airflow until the coil ices and capacity collapses. A weak capacitor or a pitted contactor turns a working system into a no-start. A thorough tune-up should include static pressure, capacitor values and amp draw, because each of those drifts before it fails. Ask which readings are included and which diagnostics are quoted separately.

The indoor half of a Chicago cooling system usually lives on the furnace. The same blower that moves heat in January moves cold air in July, through the same filter slot and the same undersized return. A filter left in for a whole season, or one rated far tighter than the ductwork can support, starves the coil. The result reads as weak cooling on the top floor.

The common fix is a thicker, higher-rated filter and a thermostat set lower. Both make the problem worse. Resistance climbs, airflow drops further, and a colder set point only lengthens the run time that is already icing the coil. That cycle can repeat for years without a single reading being taken.

Electrical parts drift instead of breaking cleanly. A run capacitor loses microfarads gradually, so the compressor starts harder each month until one hot afternoon finishes it. Contactor points pit and burn, lugs loosen with thermal cycling, and current draw creeps upward. Every one of those is a number in spring and can become a no-cool call in July.

A complete visit reads static pressure across the filter and coil, checks the blower wheel for dust loading, and fits a filter the ducts can support. Capacitor microfarads should be compared with the rating on the can, contacts inspected, and connections tightened. Where the readings show a system at the end of its service life, the conversation should turn to AC replacement instead of another round of parts.

A part that is drifting shows up as a number before it shows up as a breakdown. What can a Chicago homeowner handle between maintenance visits?

SHORT ANSWER

Filters, clearance and attention. Changing the filter on a schedule, keeping the condenser clear of cottonwood and leaves, and watching for ice, water or short cycling covers most of what does not need gauges. A spring tune-up covers the measured work, and these habits help that tune-up hold through September.

Cooling runs unattended. A thermostat holds a set point, the system cycles, and nothing asks for attention until a room is warm or a floor is wet. Chicago adds its own calendar on top of that: cottonwood in June, construction dust in a gangway, a summer of grit along a main street. Small dated habits track those conditions better than one annual visit can.

The usual pattern is nothing for months and then a flurry. A filter is changed because the air smells, the outdoor unit is hosed off because it looks dirty, the thermostat is pushed lower because the house feels warm. None of that is wrong, and none of it is measured. The habits that actually help are smaller and written on a calendar.

What helps most is recognizing the three symptoms that point to a real fault. Ice on the suction line or the indoor coil means airflow or charge, not weather. Water near the equipment means the condensate path. A system that starts and stops every few minutes is short cycling, which wears a compressor faster than steady running in heat.

The measured work belongs on the technician's side: pressures, electrical readings, static pressure and the drain. The homeowner's side is the filter, the clearance and early reporting.
