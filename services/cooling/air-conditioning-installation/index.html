How is a new air conditioner sized for a Chicago home?

SHORT ANSWER

Correct sizing comes from the building, not from the tonnage on the old condenser. Room dimensions, window area and orientation, insulation, air leakage and duct capacity set the cooling load. A sound replacement quote should follow a look at the space, the existing coil and the ductwork. Oversized equipment cools fast, shuts off early and leaves a Chicago house cold and damp rather than comfortable.

A 1920s Chicago bungalow and a gut-rehabbed two-flat on the same block can need different capacity. One has plaster walls, original windows and an uninsulated attic. The common shortcut is to match the nameplate. A failed three-ton condenser gets replaced by a three-ton condenser, even when that unit was itself a guess made decades ago by a different contractor. Short cycling is the mechanism behind many complaints about a new air conditioner. Cooling capacity is removed from the air quickly, the thermostat is satisfied, and the compressor stops before the coil has pulled much moisture out. Chicago summers are humid, so a slightly smaller unit running longer cycles often feels better than a larger one that barely runs.

What does SEER2 mean, and which efficiency level fits a Chicago home?

SHORT ANSWER

SEER2 is the seasonal cooling efficiency rating for a matched outdoor unit and indoor coil, measured at the higher airflow resistance real ductwork creates. A higher number means less electricity for the same cooling. City & Suburban installs American Standard cooling equipment, which comes in single-stage, two-stage and variable-speed configurations. A homeowner can ask for the efficiency options to be quoted side by side, so the trade-off is visible before anything is ordered.

Chicago runs a short cooling season and a long heating season, so an air conditioner here logs fewer hours than the same unit in Texas. A high-efficiency condenser still lowers a July electric bill, but the saving accumulates over roughly three hot months, not nine. That changes how much extra equipment cost makes sense.

The usual sales approach presents efficiency as a single ladder: higher number, better system, bigger price. The homeowner picks a tier from a brochure without knowing which rating applies to the pair actually being installed. Paying for a premium condenser and keeping an old indoor coil produces a system that never reaches the number on the quote.

The mechanism is in how the rating is produced. SEER2 figures are published for specific outdoor and indoor combinations tested together, and the test applies more static pressure than the older SEER method did. Efficiency therefore belongs to the pair and the ductwork, not to the box in the alley. Comfort improvements from two-stage and variable-speed operation come from longer, lower-capacity run times that remove humidity.

Only the outdoor unit appears as a line item, because swapping the coil means opening the ductwork, pulling the plenum apart and sometimes building new sheet metal transitions. The mechanism is refrigerant flow. The metering device, coil surface area and blower airflow have to match the compressor's rated capacity, or refrigerant returns to the compressor too warm or too cold. A mismatched pair can run at a fraction of its rated efficiency and strand liquid refrigerant in the coil. Cabinet width matters too, since the coil case has to sit square on the furnace it serves.

What happens to the line set, condensate drain and electrical during an AC replacement?

SHORT ANSWER

The copper line set, the condensate drain and the outdoor circuit are part of the installation, not leftovers. Lines are inspected, flushed or replaced, insulated, pressure tested and evacuated before charging. Drains are routed to an approved point and protected against blockage. The quote should confirm that these connections are included and that the charge will be recorded against the manufacturer specification for the actual line length.

In a Chicago greystone or vintage condo, the copper usually runs somewhere inconvenient. Lines are buried in a plaster chase, snaked up a light well, or stretched forty feet to a flat roof. Nobody wants to open a finished wall in July, which is exactly why the condition of that copper needs checking before the quote is signed.

The standard practice is to reuse whatever copper is there. On many jobs that is reasonable, and on some it is the only option short of demolition. The risk is that reuse gets assumed instead of tested, so an old line set carrying debris, moisture or incompatible oil becomes the weak point in otherwise new equipment.

Two mechanisms cause most of the later failures. Residual oil and acid from a burned-out compressor contaminate new refrigerant and attack the new compressor, which is why flushing or replacement matters after a burnout. Moisture left in the lines forms acid and ice at the metering device, which is why deep evacuation, not just a purge, is the standard. Line diameter and vertical rise also change the correct charge and the oil return.

A sound installation quote should confirm that the lines are pressure tested and evacuated, the suction line is insulated, the condensate is routed to an approved drain and the outdoor disconnect is connected. It depends on the condition and compatibility of the furnace or air handler. A sound blower that can move the airflow the new coil needs may stay. When the furnace is near the end of its service life or cannot supply that airflow, one combined visit avoids a second teardown of the same plenum. City & Suburban puts the price in writing upfront.

In most Chicago homes the cooling and heating equipment arrived on the same day, installed by the same contractor for the same previous owner. Twenty years later the condenser fails first, in July, while the furnace sits in the basement with the same age on its label. The decision is really about sequencing, not about one appliance.

The usual answer is to fix what broke. That is reasonable when the furnace has years left, and expensive when it does not, because the second project reopens the same ductwork. Two mobilizations mean two rounds of sheet metal work, two permit processes and two days with the system down.

When is a heat pump the better replacement for a failed air conditioner?

SHORT ANSWER

A heat pump cools exactly as an air conditioner does and can also run in reverse for heating. The moment an old condenser fails is the natural time to compare the two options. The case is strongest where the heating equipment is also aging or where there is no gas appliance. City & Suburban installs American Standard air conditioners and heat pumps.

Chicago homeowners increasingly ask about heat pumps during a cooling replacement, often after seeing an outdoor unit on a neighbor's deck that looks identical to a condenser. The question arrives mid-summer, attached to a broken air conditioner and a short decision window. It is a fair question at that moment, because both options occupy the same spot and the same line set.

The usual handling is one of two reflexes. Some contractors dismiss heat pumps outright for a Chicago winter, and others present one as the answer for every building regardless of its heating setup. Both shortcuts leave the homeowner without the comparison that actually matters: what the building needs for heat in January.

The mechanism worth knowing is simple. A heat pump is a refrigeration circuit with a reversing valve, so cooling performance and installation work are comparable to a straight air conditioner. What differs is the heating side: capacity falls as outdoor temperature drops, which raises questions about backup heat and controls. Those heating questions belong to heat pump services and to dual-fuel setups that pair a heat pump with a gas furnace.
