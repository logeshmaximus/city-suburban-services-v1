Commercial HVAC replacement questions

When has a commercial system in Chicago reached replacement rather than one more repair?

Age, failure pattern and parts availability decide it. Commercial rooftop equipment generally carries 15 to 20 years of service life. After that, efficiency and reliability tend to fall away together. One rule of thumb multiplies the equipment age by the quoted repair cost. A result above $5,000 often points to replacement. That is a rule of thumb, not a City & Suburban price. The failure count across the last two seasons belongs in the comparison as well. A bad compressor or a cracked heat exchanger moves the answer toward replacement faster than a control board does.

How is a second opinion handled when another contractor calls the system finished?

A second opinion starts with a diagnosis rather than a verdict. City & Suburban diagnoses the unit before quoting, and the exact price goes in writing before any work begins. Nothing gets condemned on age alone. A ten-year-old unit with one failed part and a clean refrigerant circuit usually belongs in service. Where replacement is the better value, the reasons should be specific: the failed component, the parts path, and the refrigerant the equipment runs. What decides the price of a commercial replacement, and when is the figure fixed?

Tonnage and the number of units carry most of it. Access comes next. A rooftop change-out that needs a crane or a stair haul costs more labor than a ground-level swap. Curb adaptation, gas and electrical capacity, controls and refrigerant line condition all move the number. So do duct transitions, permit scope, and whether the crew works after close. A replacement assessment should begin at the existing equipment and the building it serves, not at a catalog. Nameplate data, the condition of the gas and electrical connections, condensate routing, and the load the space actually carries all belong in that review. Equipment that was wrong for the space in 2005 should not be copied in 2026. How long is a building without conditioning while the equipment is swapped?

No fixed duration fits every building. A single change-out often lands inside one working day, while larger scopes with several pieces of equipment run longer. Access decides much of it. A crane lift or a stair haul adds time that a ground-level swap does not. The outage window should be confirmed in writing before the exchange, so the shutdown is scheduled rather than discovered. Can replacement be phased across several units so trading continues?

Yes. On a multi-unit roof, phasing is usually the right call. One unit comes out at a time. Only part of the floor plate loses conditioning on a given day, and the units left in place carry what capacity remains. Sequencing follows the building's calendar: kitchens, server rooms and tenant suites get scheduled around their own peak hours.

Which symptoms mean a commercial unit should be shut down, not run another season?

A gas odor or any carbon monoxide reading means leaving the building first and calling 911 or the gas utility from outside. Nobody should restart, open or inspect the equipment in that situation. Soot around the burners belongs in the same group. In buildings with flats above a storefront, the Illinois Carbon Monoxide Alarm Detector Act sets the alarm requirement for those dwelling units. A second group is mechanical: a cracked heat exchanger, repeated breaker trips, scorched contactors and a short-cycling compressor. Equipment in that condition goes off and stays off until a technician has it open. City & Suburban offers same-day service when the schedule allows for failures in that second group.

Which Chicago businesses does replacement suit, and which should wait?

Replacement suits buildings whose units are past 15 years, failing in consecutive seasons, or running a phased-out refrigerant with no parts path. It also suits owners facing a tenant fit-out or a sale, where documented new equipment removes a negotiating point. A two-year-old unit with one failed part is the wrong case. That work belongs with commercial HVAC repair. A building with no system at all needs commercial HVAC installation instead. The full scope sits on the commercial HVAC overview for the building.

What efficiency change should a business expect from new equipment?

The gain depends on where the old equipment sat on the curve. A gas section at 80% AFUE turns much less of each therm into delivered heat than a 96% unit does. Modern compressors also stage or modulate instead of running flat out on every call. Controls matter as much as the box. A change-out that keeps a 1990s thermostat and leaking ducts gives part of the gain straight back. City & Suburban installs American Standard equipment. Utility incentive programs for high-efficiency commercial equipment exist in Chicago and change often, so current terms are worth confirming before the order. Equipment is also a business expense, and depreciation treatment is a question for the building's accountant.

Does the refrigerant change affect a commercial replacement in Chicago?

It often decides the comparison. R-22 is no longer produced. Equipment still running it depends on reclaimed supply. A single large leak can end the repair path on its own. Much new equipment now uses A2L refrigerants, but not all of it does. Where an A2L unit is chosen, line sizing, leak detection and handling rules can differ from the R-410A generation. Which rules apply depends on the equipment category, the refrigerant selected, the current transition rules and the maker's instructions. Reusing an existing line set is a question of condition and compatibility, not a given. A contaminated circuit should not be carried into new equipment.

How does the Chicago Heat Ordinance affect replacement timing in a mixed-use building?

Where the landlord supplies the heat, the Chicago Heat Ordinance sets minimum indoor temperatures from September 15 to June 1. The floor is 68 degrees from 8:30 a.m. to 10:30 p.m., and 66 degrees overnight, under Municipal Code 13-196-410. The code lists exceptions, so it reads as the general rule. A winter change-out in a building with dwelling units therefore has to be sequenced so heat is not off beyond those minimums. In practice that means a single-day exchange, or temporary heat planned in advance. Planning the shutoff sequence first is what keeps the building inside the ordinance.

Does the new equipment have to match the brand coming out?

No. City & Suburban installs American Standard equipment on replacements, and matching the outgoing brand is not required. What must match is the application: tonnage, cabinet and curb fit, gas input, electrical service and refrigerant type. Control compatibility with whatever stays in place matters just as much. Where only part of a system changes, the existing air handler or evaporator coil has to pair with the new outdoor section. Otherwise the rated efficiency never shows up in the building. City & Suburban repairs all makes and models, so a mixed-brand building stays serviceable through a staged replacement.
