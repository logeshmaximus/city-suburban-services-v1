Equipment types

Follow the air path. Match the duct to the room.

Galvanized sheet metal trunk and branch

The trunk carries air toward several spaces; branches divide that airflow between rooms. A build-out changes the destinations. Retain sound metal where the route and size fit the new layout; isolate damaged sections instead of replacing the entire distribution system.

Spiral and oval duct

Route, clearance and the required airflow determine the duct shape. A ceiling plan needs room for the duct and its connections, with access for later inspection. Material choice belongs in the project scope before fabrication begins.

Flexible duct runouts

Runouts connect the distribution route to individual outlets. A crushed or sagging connection restricts delivery even when the main route remains sound. Flexible duct is not a substitute for a designed trunk; suitability depends on the application and project requirements.

Internal liner and external wrap

Liner addresses sound inside the air path; external wrap insulates the duct from surrounding conditions. The choice follows the noise or thermal problem. Insulation alone cannot correct an undersized branch or replace a missing return path.

Volume dampers and VAV boxes

Volume dampers set branch airflow, while VAV boxes regulate delivery to served spaces. Access matters after a ceiling closes. Record final settings so a later comfort complaint can be compared with the completed balance rather than corrected by guesswork.

Diffusers, grilles and the return path

Supply outlets deliver air into rooms; return paths allow it to leave. New partitions change both routes. The distribution connection to an air handler cannot compensate for a closed office separated from its return. The cutaway illustrates ductwork and zoning.

Commercial ductwork questions

What does commercial ductwork design and repair include in a Chicago building?

Commercial duct work covers everything between the fan and the registers. That means trunk and branch sizing, damper positions, sealed seams, insulation, return air paths and noise control. Design work redraws the layout when walls move or a space changes use. Repair work fixes runs that leak, sag, sit crushed above a ceiling grid, or were never sized for the load. It fits offices, retail units, restaurants, clinics, small industrial spaces, and condo or association common areas. City & Suburban coverage runs to 21 service areas, 13 Chicago neighborhoods and 8 suburbs, alongside the rest of the commercial HVAC scope in the same building.

Is this duct cleaning, or duct repair and redesign?

Duct cleaning removes dust and debris from inside existing runs and changes nothing about the layout. Duct repair and design change the system itself: sealing joints, replacing a crushed section, resizing a branch, moving a run, setting dampers. A clean duct that was never sized right still starves the same room. The service-call coupon on this page excludes duct cleaning, so a cleaning search lands in the wrong place. Routine checks and cleaning sit on duct maintenance, and a single leaky run in a house or two-flat sits on duct repair. How is uneven temperature between rooms or floors actually corrected?

Order matters more than equipment. Walls move during a build-out while the registers stay put. A trunk drawn for an open floor plate then ends up feeding closed offices. A new demising wall can cut off the return path a floor depended on. Airflow comes first, because a room starved of air cannot be fixed at the controls. Balancing dampers get set, undersized branches get resized, and blocked or missing returns get opened. Only after that does zoning or a control change make sense. What are the signs that a duct run is leaking or badly undersized?

Rooms that never reach setpoint while the equipment runs without stopping. A system loud at the unit and weak at the register. Dust arriving in a space that should be clean. Doors that pull hard against the frame or slam shut. Leaking duct spills conditioned air into a shaft or plenum nobody occupies. Undersized runs raise static pressure until the fan moves less air than the design assumed. City & Suburban diagnoses duct faults before anything is quoted. How is a commercial duct design actually produced?

A sound design starts with measurement, not a drawing. The survey should record room sizes, occupancy, equipment capacity and the runs that already exist. Airflow at each register and the static pressure across the fan, filter and coil show what the existing duct can carry. Duct sizing then follows from the air each space needs, and trunk, branches and return are drawn to carry it at a speed that stays quiet. Damper positions should be recorded, so the balanced settings can be checked next season. A design proposal should state which measurements it rests on. Can noisy ducts in an office or retail space be quieted?

Usually, and often without new equipment. Duct noise has three sources. Air moves too fast through an undersized trunk, bare metal radiates fan noise, and vibration travels through hangers or a roof curb. Resizing, lining and isolation address each one. Scope, access and timing decide it. The count and length of runs matters, and so does how much duct sits above a finished ceiling. When does a duct fault become urgent rather than a comfort complaint?

Three cases call for prompt attention. Return air drawn from a room with gas-fired equipment can starve combustion air and cause backdrafting. That risk matters most in a mixed-use building over occupied apartments. If a carbon monoxide alarm sounds or gas is smelled, everyone leaves the building first and calls 911, or the gas utility for a gas odor, from outside. A kitchen exhaust hood without matched makeup air puts a whole space under negative pressure. A collapsed or disconnected return in January leaves tenants cold. Does changing ductwork in a Chicago building need a permit?

Often, and scale decides the path. Many mechanical alterations in Chicago need a City of Chicago permit, and a like-for-like duct repair may be treated differently from a new layout tied to a build-out, so confirm the requirement for the specific scope with the City before work is scheduled. What about fire and smoke dampers in an occupied building?

Rated walls, floors and shafts are built to hold back fire and smoke, and duct that penetrates one is normally fitted with a fire or smoke damper at the crossing. Those dampers close on heat or on a smoke signal, and the opening has to stay accessible so the damper can be inspected and reset. Rerouting a branch or adding a supply through a corridor wall touches that barrier, which makes a route change a code question and not only a sheet metal question.
