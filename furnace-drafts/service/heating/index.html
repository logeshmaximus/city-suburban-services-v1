What do furnace efficiency ratings like 80% and 95% mean?

The rating, called AFUE, is the share of fuel that becomes heat over a season. An 80% furnace turns 80% of its gas into heat and vents up a chimney. A 95% furnace also pulls heat from its exhaust, so it vents through plastic pipe and needs a drain. In a long Chicago winter, the higher class costs more up front and less to run.

Which heating jobs need a City of Chicago permit?

In Chicago, replacing heating or cooling equipment, such as a furnace, boiler or heat pump, requires a City building permit. Routine repair, diagnosis and maintenance usually do not. The permit puts the gas, venting and electrical work on record with the Department of Buildings. An offer to replace equipment without a permit skips that step.

What is the difference between a furnace, a boiler and a heat pump?

A furnace burns gas to heat air, and a blower pushes that air through ducts. A boiler heats water or makes steam, which carries heat through pipes to radiators, baseboard or floor tubing. A heat pump burns no fuel. It uses electricity to move heat from outdoor air indoors in winter, then reverses to cool in summer. Many Chicago buildings suit only one or two of these.

How long do home heating systems last?

Gas furnaces often last 15 to 20 years. Cast-iron boilers often run for decades, while high-efficiency boilers usually have shorter lives. Air-source heat pumps and ductless mini-splits often last 10 to 15 years, partly because the same unit works in both seasons. Yearly service, clean filters and correct sizing stretch any system's life, while short-cycling and neglect cut it short.

Do Illinois homes need carbon monoxide alarms?

Yes. Under the Illinois Carbon Monoxide Alarm Detector Act, homes with fuel-burning heat, a fireplace or an attached garage need CO alarms. An alarm must be within 15 feet of every room used for sleeping. A furnace or boiler with a cracked part or a blocked vent can leak carbon monoxide without any smell. A yearly heating check is a good time to test each alarm.

When does a heating problem need a technician, and what should a repair visit look like?

SHORT ANSWER

A heating problem needs a technician when the system stops, short-cycles, makes new noises or leaves rooms cold after basic checks. A gas smell or a carbon monoxide alarm means leave the building first, then call 911 or the gas utility from outside. On a City & Suburban repair visit, the technician finds the cause first and puts the exact price in writing before work starts.

Heating trouble in Chicago clusters in the first cold weeks of the season and during deep freezes, when systems run almost nonstop. A homeowner can safely check the temperature setting, the circuit breaker, the filter and any snow over outdoor vent pipes. Anything past those checks belongs to a trained technician.

A rushed service call often replaces whichever part failed and moves on. The same fault returns weeks later, usually on a colder night. Another common pattern skips repair and steers the homeowner toward a new system. Either way, the homeowner pays twice.

What gets missed is that many shutdowns are safety controls at work. A clogged filter can overheat a furnace until a switch cuts the burner. A blocked vent or low water can stop a boiler. Swapping the part that tripped does nothing about the reason it tripped.

What is hybrid dual-fuel heating, and what is the balance point?

SHORT ANSWER

A hybrid dual-fuel system combines an electric heat pump and a gas furnace in one ducted system. The controls switch from one to the other at a set outdoor temperature tied to the balance point. The heat pump covers mild days, and the furnace handles deep cold. City & Suburban can pair an American Standard heat pump with a sound existing furnace and puts the full price in writing first.

Many Chicago homes already own half of a dual-fuel system. A gas furnace sits in the basement, and an aging central air conditioner sits outside. The Cook County Assessor counts 133,080 houses and small residential buildings with central air in the city's eight townships. Each has ducts and an outdoor pad.

When central air fails, the usual approach installs another air conditioner and leaves the furnace to carry every heating hour. Going all-electric is the other extreme. In an older Chicago home, that can mean a larger heat pump, electric heat strips and a panel upgrade.

The balance point is the detail many installers skip. One version is thermal: the outdoor temperature below which the heat pump alone cannot match the home's heat loss. The other is economic: the temperature at which gas becomes the cheaper fuel. The switchover setting should follow those numbers and move when energy prices move.
